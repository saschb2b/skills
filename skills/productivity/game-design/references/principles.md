# Design Moves Worth Stealing

The principles that recur across the dissected games and generalize past their genre. Each is a move you can apply, the reason it works, and the caveat, because none of these is universal. Both work, for different games. These are the opinionated layer; the neutral pattern definitions are in [patterns.md](patterns.md), and the academic backing for several is in [frameworks.md](frameworks.md).

## Structure and identity

- **Pick one dialectic and restate it fractally.** Choose a single central tension ("speed vs accuracy", "greed vs survival", "specialize vs adapt") and make every screen a restatement of it. Players intuit the principle once and apply it everywhere; the game feels coherent. The most transferable move in the set. Source: Slay the Spire, Moonlighter 2, Mega Man Battle Network.
- **Constraint produces identity.** A small slot count drawn from a large pool forces builds to be distinct. About four slots is the repeated sweet spot; six is usually too many, two too few. Source: Balatro (5 of 150+ Jokers), Hollow Knight (11 notches), Pokemon (4 moves).
- **Opportunity cost must be felt, and visible.** Every loadout choice should foreclose another, and the player must be able to see what they are giving up. If they cannot see the sacrifice, the budget does not bite. Source: Sparklite, Moonlighter 2, Nier Automata (the HUD itself is in the budget).
- **Name failure and persistence in the fiction's voice.** The cheapest ludonarrative move available: a wipe screen that says "Expedition Failed" instead of "Game Over", a continue button that says "We Continue", a counter banner that names the group rather than the hero. It costs a string table and does real thematic work, because the failure and retry loop is where the player spends the most emotional time. Source: Expedition 33; Dark Souls' "YOU DIED" as the tone-setting ancestor.
- **Build on one verb, then deepen it, not widen it.** Pick one core verb and make every enemy, level, and item pose a question about that verb; let upgrades extend the verb rather than add parallel ones. One polished core ability beats five mediocre ones, you get coherence for free, and scope stays sane. A cousin move buys the same coherence in the fiction: reuse one story-object across systems instead of inventing a new abstract resource. The caveat is real: if the verb is the whole game, thin enemy and level authoring is fatal, so budget the content side even though the systems side is small. Source: Mina the Hollower (Hollowing is dodge, traversal, and attack opener, and upgrades fire through it or change where it goes; the Spark is MacGuffin, gate, checkpoint, and death-buffer at once).

## Power and progression

- **Meta-progression unlocks variety, not power.** For replay-heavy games, the next run should be more interesting, not easier. New options, modifiers, and starting states, yes; bigger base stats, no. Caveat: a deliberate power ramp is right for a game that wants you to eventually win and then stop (Hades). Pick a stance and be consistent. Source: Slay the Spire, Balatro.
- **Costed power forces commitment, not collection.** Give run-defining loot a real drawback so each run gets a direction. Uncosted upgrades flatten variety because every great run ends up the same shape. Use soft costs (opportunity cost) freely and hard costs (a permanent ban on a whole system) sparingly. Source: Slay the Spire boss relics, Path of Exile keystones, BotW heart-vs-stamina.
- **A permanent fork makes players into kinds of players.** A meta choice you cannot respec ("more hearts or more stamina") turns a loadout into an identity. If you let players respec, it is a loadout, not a choice. Source: Breath of the Wild, Cult of the Lamb doctrines.
- **Augment, never replace.** New progression systems should add an orthogonal axis, not supersede an old one. Replacement creates dead content; augmentation creates depth. A new system that is just "more damage" competes with the existing one and forces you to deprecate it. Source: Warframe (held the line for over a decade), Path of Exile leagues.
- **How long can you keep teaching.** In a long game, engagement past hour 30 comes from learning a system that just unlocked, not from more of the same. A system introduced at hour H needs roughly H more hours of content to justify it, late reveals should be narratively earned, and returning players need a tutorial log. Source: Xenoblade, Nier Automata, Warframe; Expedition 33 gets the same energy from party members who arrive as new mini-games deep into the campaign.
- **Layer a universal passive language under unique kits.** A two-layer build architecture (one shared passive system every character speaks, private subsystems that keep each character mechanically distinct) buys multiplicative build variety at additive content cost: every passive you design works for the whole roster, every kit stays a distinct identity. Caveat: a budget system this expressive needs loadout presets and management UI from day one; if the build system is the endgame, its management screen is endgame content. Source: Expedition 33 (Luminas under six kits; Lumina Sets arrived months late in a patch), Balatro-scale combinatorics with a party-RPG face.
- **Equipment that teaches is stronger than equipment that buffs.** Learning an item's passive by fighting with it equipped turns the inventory into a curriculum: optimal play is rotating unlearned gear, so players explore the catalog by playing well instead of despite it, and any game with equipment and passives can retrofit it with one counter per item. Caveat: the learned library needs its own budget or the endgame is a kitchen sink. Source: Final Fantasy IX (the origin), Expedition 33 (Pictos and Luminas, the modernized form); the pattern entry is equip-to-learn in [patterns.md](patterns.md).

## Combat and fairness

- **Give the defender a verb.** Whatever the combat structure, make the player do something when they are attacked; the dead time between "my turn" and "my turn again" is where genre fatigue lives. Expedition 33 proves the payoff inside the most turn-based of frames: every enemy attack is dodged, parried, or jumped by hand, and the enemy's turn becomes the most alert moment of the fight. Even one dodge button during enemy actions changes the entire feel of menu combat. Caveat: if defense is mandatory, audit the or-die threshold. One-shot damage converts a skill bonus into a skill gate, and the build system stops mattering for anyone who cannot execute; keep at least one honest build-based answer (tank stats, shields, revives) viable at every difficulty, or admit the game is an action game. Source: Expedition 33.
- **Pay for risk in resources, not just survival.** When two defensive options exist, make the harder one an economic engine, not just a better shield: Expedition 33's parry is tighter than its dodge and generates the AP that funds skills, so the economy itself teaches mastery with no tutorial text. Caveat: an economy that pays only the hard input makes it strictly dominant for anyone who can execute it, which is the game's own most-criticized dynamic; pay both options and tilt the rate instead of zeroing one. Source: Expedition 33.
- **Telegraph honestly; a telegraph is a promise.** Show what is coming so combat is a decision, not a coin flip. This moves randomness off the enemy and onto the player's own kit. The honesty is non-negotiable: a telegraph that lies or omits is worse than none. It is a promise about *what*, though, not necessarily *when*: Expedition 33 keeps attack type perfectly legible while making the timing deliberately theatrical, a tunable difficulty dial that produced both its mastery ceiling and its accessibility complaints. If you turn that dial, budget the assist tools (practice or retry modes, a timing-assist option decoupled from the difficulty label, tells authored on readable silhouettes) from day one. Source: Slay the Spire, Mega Man Battle Network, Xenoblade Vision, Hollow Knight, Expedition 33.
- **Subtract; make skipping a real option.** A tighter, smaller kit usually beats a bigger one, and removing should be as rewarding as adding. Treat "take nothing" as a first-class choice in any reward draft; it changes the decision from "which is best" to "is any of these worth my limited slot". Source: Slay the Spire, Mega Man Battle Network, Balatro.
- **Graduate the death penalty; make failure recoverable.** A death economy that buffers the first mistakes and only bites sustained failure keeps tension without punishing learning, which retains players far better than lose-everything-on-death. The shape: a buffer resource that absorbs early deaths, a recoverable drop at the place you fell, and a mercy rule (a level-up reclaims it). It is the Souls bloodstain or Nier chip-drop minus the cruelty, and it is cheap to build (a counter, a dropped pickup with a position, a reclaim hook). If you ship punishing defaults, budget the systemic softener and an accessibility floor first, or hard difficulty reads as broken rather than hardcore. Source: Mina the Hollower (Sparks), Nier Automata (chip-drop).

## Systems and economy

- **Write rules, not encounters.** A small system of universal, multiplicative rules generates more emergent content than a large team can hand-author. Multiplicative interactions beat additive content. Source: Breath of the Wild's chemistry engine.
- **Every currency is a verb.** Prefer currencies that each do something (perform an operation, gate a craft) over an abstract pile of gold; inflation control then lives in the system rather than being bolted on. Source: Path of Exile.
- **The chase is the game.** For loot-driven games, a perfect item must be reachable but rarely reached. Determinism is a bug to nerf in regulated doses; if you close the chase, you remove the engine. Caveat: this needs a working trade or second economy to absorb the imperfect items. Source: Path of Exile, Warframe Rivens.
- **Friction can be identity, but it needs tooling even when it is the point.** Deliberate inconvenience (manual trade, earned cartography, hidden math, no minimap) can be the point and filter for the audience you want. Caveat: it also filters out players you might have wanted, so choose it on purpose, not by neglect, and fund what the removed convenience was doing. Removing the map only reads as design if landmark-driven, silhouette-readable levels carry the navigation load the UI dropped; pair it with samey corridors and invisible walls and the same friction reads as jank. Source: Path of Exile, Hollow Knight, Pokemon; Expedition 33's no-minimap stance as the half-paid version.
- **Push consumables forward; do not let players hoard them.** A scarce, powerful one-shot feels too precious to spend, so players save the megalixir for a boss they never reach. That is loss aversion, not strategy, and it punishes the weaker players the item was meant to help. Fix it by making consumables plentiful and cheap, putting them on a cooldown instead of consuming them, making them self-replenish, or spreading the effect across many items so no single one feels precious. Keep the decision, remove the hoarding. Source: Dark Souls (Estus refills at every bonfire, over Demon's Souls finite healing grass), Diablo 3 (one potion on a cooldown), Expedition 33 (tints refill at every expedition flag, keeping items per-attempt instead of per-playthrough). The full inventory treatment is in [inventory.md](inventory.md).

## Process and scope

- **Pick a stance: bounded or infinite.** A narrative-leaning game usually wants a bounded arc with opt-in escalation; a grind-as-endgame game wants infinite escalation. Decide which, rather than drifting. Source: Hades and Sparklite (bounded) versus Warframe (infinite).
- **Constraints are a brief.** A publisher or hardware limit often becomes the game's identity. Treat the constraint as a design prompt, not just an obstacle, and prefer a constraint that also solves a design problem: Mina the Hollower's Game Boy Color palette and chunky grid force the clean silhouettes and legible telegraphs that timing combat needs, so the limit does the art direction and the readability at once. Just remember a retro look is a promise about feel, not pixels, so the input latency, animation snap, and audio punch have to match the reference. Source: Nier Automata (a "more content" note became the multi-route structure), Warframe (a rejected pitch became free-to-play trade), BotW (Wii U hardware drove rules over content), Mina the Hollower (a self-imposed GBC constraint as a legibility device).
- **Scale the math to your team, do not scale up.** A 4x4 grid is enough, a 5 square kilometer world wants about ten points of interest rather than a hundred. Down-scale ambitious moves to a budget you can actually tune and ship. Source: the recurring indie reading of AAA systems across the catalog.
- **Design more than you ship, and prune ruthlessly.** Most games ship too much. Generate many ideas and let half of what you prototype get cut; anything that does not earn its slot is noise. Pair this with tracking something real (pick rates, win rates, completion) over another round of playtester opinions. Source: Slay the Spire (Mega Crit's metric-driven pruning).
- **Seedable RNG buys community for free.** If a game has any randomness, make it seedable from day one and add a daily-seeded mode with a leaderboard: everyone plays the same seed today, the leaderboard reveals skill. Low cost, high engagement, the kind of community a patch cannot manufacture (Wordle proved it). Source: Slay the Spire's daily climb. Pair it with quiet anti-streak pity systems (bias drops upward after a dry spell) so random rewards rarely feel malicious; see [systems.md](systems.md).
- **Decide your break-the-game policy before launch.** In a single-player game, the damage ceiling is either content (then test it like content) or the player's toy (then hand them the dials on day one). Drifting between the two is what hurts: Expedition 33's opt-in cap-removing Lumina was a great idea, and the untested billion-damage build that one-shot the final superboss was that idea unowned; the recovery (a pre-announced nerf, then player-set challenge modifiers) was excellent but months late. Single-player balance is a service to build diversity, not a leash. Source: Expedition 33; the philosophy contrast lives in vision-driven-iteration in [patterns.md](patterns.md).
- **Climb a difficulty staircase, do not ship a slider.** For any game with a "did you win" loop, replay depth comes from a staircase of stacking single-constraint modifiers the player climbs one rung at a time, not an Easy-to-Hard slider chosen once. Each rung is a digestible new lesson and players self-pace. Source: Slay the Spire Ascension, Hades Heat, Balatro Stakes; the pattern is in [patterns.md](patterns.md).

## The meta-principle

These are heuristics, not laws, and several of them contradict each other on purpose (vision-driven versus metric-driven; bounded versus infinite; friction versus convenience). The skill is knowing which one your game wants. Hold them as questions to ask, not boxes to check, and be ready to argue with any of them.
